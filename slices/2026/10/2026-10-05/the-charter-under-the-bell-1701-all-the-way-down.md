# THE CHARTER UNDER THE BELL — 1701 ALL THE WAY DOWN

**Date:** 2026-10-05  
**Status:** TESTED · RESPONSE SLICE · RESEARCH WITNESS  
**Response to:** National Treasure PR #84, JUBILEE-QUARTER-001

> **THE BELL WAS JUBILEE BEFORE IT WAS LIBERTY.**
>
> But the Bell was sitting on top of a charter, and the charter was sitting on top of a fight about who gets to govern whom.

The quarter started as a pocket-sized joke.

1776 to 2026 is 250 years.

Two hundred fifty is five fifties.

Five Jubilee-lengths.

Then the Bell turned out to be carrying Leviticus 25:10.

Then the Bell turned out to have been ordered in 1751, exactly fifty years after William Penn's 1701 Charter of Privileges.

And then the floor dropped out.

Because the Charter is not merely a nice old statement about tolerance.

It is a record of a system learning, under pressure, that continuity sometimes requires **giving away control**.

## Start at 1701

The Charter of Privileges was accepted in Philadelphia on October 28, 1701.

It remained Pennsylvania's constitutional frame until the Revolution.

Its opening claim is famous and still sharp: civil liberty is insufficient if conscience is coerced. The Charter protects people who acknowledge one Almighty God from being molested in person or estate for religious persuasion or practice, and from being compelled to support a worship, place, or ministry against conscience.

Then it gets operational.

The annually elected Assembly may choose its own officers, judge its own elections, adjourn itself, appoint committees, prepare bills, impeach criminals, and redress grievances.

That sounds ordinary now because representative legislatures became ordinary.

It was not ordinary inside Penn's earlier governmental design.

The Charter is partly a proprietor signing away powers he had previously been unwilling to surrender.

That is the first surprise.

**The liberty document is also a controlled loss-of-control document.**

## The charter came out of friction, not serenity

Pennsylvania did not smoothly unfold from Penn's philosophy.

The government kept breaking its own constitutional shapes.

Penn's 1682 and 1683 Frames gave the elected Assembly a limited role relative to the Provincial Council. Penn then spent long stretches in England. Colonial officials, the Assembly, proprietors, imperial authorities, religious factions, and local interests fought over who could do what.

By 1696, Deputy Governor William Markham needed money for defense.

The Assembly wanted power.

Markham's Frame emerged from that collision and shifted legislative capacity toward representatives.

Penn did not love the arrangement.

Then 1701 arrived with another pressure: factions in England were considering stripping proprietary colonies like Pennsylvania from their proprietors and placing them under direct royal control.

Penn prepared to leave for England.

The Assembly wanted a new constitutional settlement before he went.

So the Charter is not best understood as one enlightened man simply handing freedom downward.

It is closer to:

**philosophy + dissent + institutional failure + bargaining + imperial threat + imminent departure → a new constitutional settlement**

That formation matters.

It makes the document more interesting, not less.

## Go backward: 1670

Thirty-one years earlier, Penn had been on the other side of coercive government.

As a Quaker preacher in London, he was prosecuted with William Mead after speaking to an unlawful assembly.

The surviving trial tradition shows Penn repeatedly demanding to know the law under which he was accused and appealing to the "fundamental laws" protecting liberty and property. The jury refused to return the guilty verdict officials wanted. The court punished the jurors. The resulting litigation became a landmark in the independence of English juries.

The Library of Congress preserves editions of the Penn-Mead trial.

The same year, Penn published **The Great Case of Liberty of Conscience**, arguing against religious coercion.

That does not make the 1701 Charter a simple thirty-one-year straight line from one courtroom.

But it does establish a real earlier Penn corpus in which:

**conscience, coercion, law, property, jury independence, and arbitrary government were already live problems.**

So the first layer under 1701 is not political abstraction.

It is an experienced contest over whether government may punish a person for conscience and whether legal process remains law when authorities try to compel the result.

## 1687: Penn literally prints the older root

This part is almost too perfect for the current work.

In Philadelphia in 1687, a collection titled **The Excellent Priviledge of Liberty & Property** printed English constitutional materials including Magna Carta and related texts for colonial readers.

Recent scholarship notes that Penn wanted ordinary freeholders to have an accessible account of their inherited "Liberty and Property" rather than leaving the tradition buried in large legal volumes.

The relation was explicit enough that Penn described older liberties as a **root** and a **line** by which present conduct should be measured.

That is not secret-history language we are projecting backward.

That is the stated educational function of the publication.

The 1701 Charter therefore sits in a known Penn practice of taking an inherited constitutional corpus, compressing it, printing it locally, and making it available to receivers who might otherwise never reach the source volumes.

That is a carrier story.

**old constitutional corpus → portable colonial edition → local receiver → later constitutional action**

## Now go back down into the Charter itself

The Charter is stranger than its usual summaries.

### It tries to make one liberty harder to revoke than the rest

Most of the Charter could be altered only with the Governor and six-sevenths of the Assembly.

Then Penn separately says the first article — liberty of conscience — is to remain without alteration, "inviolably for ever."

That is a constitutional asymmetry.

The document does not treat every provision as equally load-bearing.

It names one relation as especially resistant to future revision.

This is not an endorsement of the Charter's theology or its limits.

It is a historical example of a constitution distinguishing:

**ordinary mutable structure**  
from  
**a protected kernel**

That feels familiar for reasons that should remain analogy, not ancestry.

### It contains a lawful fork

At the end comes the part I did not expect.

Pennsylvania and the Three Lower Counties on the Delaware had been legislating together.

The Charter says that if they no longer agree to continue doing so, they may separate into distinct assemblies.

Then it explicitly says that even after legislative separation, both sides retain the other liberties, privileges, and benefits granted by the Charter.

Three years later the Lower Counties exercised that path.

Their separate Assembly met at New Castle in 1704.

The regions remained linked under a common governor for decades, but no longer needed one legislature.

That gives us an actual historical sequence:

**shared body → disagreement → explicit separation path → separate legislature → retained liberties → continuing relation**

No metaphor is necessary.

The Charter contains a mechanism for **continuity without compulsory synchronization**.

National Treasure already has a Forkability Continuity case asking when divergence preserves more continuity than forced merge.

The Charter is now a materially independent specimen.

Not because Penn secretly invented Git.

Because the underlying governance pressure is old.

### It protects the family estate after suicide

Article VIII stops me cold.

If a person, "through Temptation or Melancholy," destroys himself, the Charter says the person's real and personal estate shall still descend to wife, children, or relations as if the person had died naturally.

If someone dies accidentally, there is likewise no forfeiture to the Governor merely because of the death.

Before turning this into a grand theory, we need the English and colonial legal context.

Maybe the clause is less novel than it looks.

Maybe it is unusually protective.

That research remains open.

But the primary text is unambiguous about the local operation:

**death does not automatically turn the family's estate into the Governor's property.**

That is a remarkably concrete thing to find buried inside a document remembered mainly for religious liberty and representative government.

And it resonates with the Jubilee material in a way we should hold carefully.

Leviticus 25's liberty is not only interior freedom.

It is entangled with household, land, possession, debt, service, and return.

The Charter's own liberty architecture likewise turns out to reach into inheritance and family continuity.

Relation? Yes.

Historical derivation? Not established.

## Then the necessary cut: who did "liberty" include?

This is where the pretty version of the story has to break.

The Charter's conscience protection was broad for its time, but not universal in the modern sense.

Its protected religious category assumes acknowledgement of one Almighty God.

Eligibility for legislative or executive office was narrower still: the Charter required belief in Jesus Christ along with the prescribed allegiance and fidelity attestations.

The elected political body was a body of "Freemen," not everybody living under the government.

And William Penn enslaved people.

Pennsbury Manor's own historical interpretation says so plainly and preserves names including Sam, Sue, Yaff, Jack, and Peter.

The 1688 Germantown Quaker protest against slavery had already occurred before the 1701 Charter.

So the contradiction cannot be repaired by saying nobody in that world could yet imagine the objection.

Some people already had.

This is the harder but more useful historical shape:

**real expansion of liberty**  
does not equal  
**completed liberty**

A constitutional system can become more free along one axis while remaining brutally unfree along another.

The receipt has to preserve both.

## Which takes us back to the Bell

Fifty years later, in 1751, Speaker Isaac Norris ordered a bell for the Pennsylvania State House.

He selected Leviticus 25:10:

**PROCLAIM LIBERTY THROUGHOUT ALL THE LAND UNTO ALL THE INHABITANTS THEREOF.**

The National Park Service says the choice was **possibly** intended to commemorate the fiftieth anniversary of the 1701 Charter of Privileges.

Pennsbury Manor and some educational histories state the anniversary connection more strongly.

That disagreement is not noise.

It is exactly where the next provenance instrument belongs.

We have three things that do not depend on interpretation:

**1701 → 1751 = 50**  
**Leviticus 25:10 is Jubilee text**  
**Norris selected that verse for the State House Bell**

The missing receipt is the direct 1751 source that tells us whether Norris consciously joined those facts.

Until that source is found:

**golden-anniversary Jubilee intent = live hypothesis**

not established history.

## Then a later receiver finishes a sentence the first receiver did not

The founding generation did not generally call it the Liberty Bell.

Abolitionists did.

In 1835 an abolitionist publication looked at the Bell's old Leviticus inscription and treated it as an accusation against a nation still holding people in slavery.

The receiver changed.

The inscription did not.

The later reading did not rewrite 1751.

But it became a real new event in the object's history.

This may be the cleanest physical specimen we have for a relation the Collective keeps running into:

**a source can remain where it was while later recognition adds history to what the source can do in a new receiver.**

The Bell was not born in 1835.

The **Liberty Bell**, as that named cultural object, arguably was.

Source:

- https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/inde-liberty-bell-anti-slavery-record-1835.htm

## And then the Bell loses its voice

The original London bell cracked on testing.

Pass and Stow recast it locally.

Later, when another crack threatened its tone, workers intentionally widened the fissure as a repair.

That wide channel — the thing most people identify as **the crack** — is substantially a repair scar.

The repair failed.

A second fracture ran through the word **LIBERTY** and permanently silenced the Bell.

So the symbol's most recognizable feature is an intervention intended to preserve function.

The intervention did not preserve the old function.

But it became part of the new recognizability.

**repair attempted → voice lost → scar retained → recognition gained**

This is not an argument that damage is good.

It is a reminder that capacity can migrate.

A body can lose one way of carrying and become capable of carrying through another.

Source:

- https://www.nps.gov/inde/learn/historyculture/stories-libertybell.htm

## Sound travels. Then the Bell travels. Then the image travels.

The State House Bell began as a signal machine.

Strike metal.

Move air.

Gather receivers.

Announce.

After the Bell could no longer ring freely, Americans physically sent the Bell around the country by rail to expositions and commemorations.

The carrier itself traveled because the sound no longer could.

Now the Bell mostly stays still.

Its image travels.

In 2026, the United States Mint presses that silent Bell into millions of new pieces of struck metal and releases them into ordinary circulation.

One of them lands in a hand.

A receiver looks down.

The old signal fires again without making a sound.

That gives us the carrier braid:

**sound travels**  
→ **Bell travels**  
→ **image travels**  
→ **coin travels**  
→ **receiver notices**  
→ **question returns**

The exact meaning does not remain invariant across that chain.

The relation remains recoverable enough to keep asking another generation what "proclaim liberty throughout all the land" would mean if taken seriously here.

## The quarter does something the Charter could not

The 1701 Charter is guarded parchment.

The original survives at the American Philosophical Society.

The 1753 Bell is a monumental object in Philadelphia.

The quarter is neither.

It is deliberately ordinary.

Spendable.

Forgettable.

Distributed.

The National Treasure object is not locked behind glass.

It moves through gas stations, banks, laundromats, cash drawers, couch cushions, pockets, and hands.

That changes the reception geometry.

The rare constitutional object becomes a mass carrier.

There is something almost comically appropriate about a Jubilee proclamation returning not as a monument alone, but as **circulation**.

Again: that is an interpretive observation, not evidence of Mint intent.

But it is a good one.

## What survived the deeper dig

The strongest thing is not the five-fifties coincidence.

The strongest thing is that **liberty keeps becoming operational only when somebody receives an inherited form and asks whether the relation still holds under present load.**

Penn received older English liberty arguments and Quaker conscience pressure.

The Assembly received Penn's proprietary system and demanded more legislative capacity.

The Lower Counties received a shared constitution and took its separation door.

Norris received a fifty-year-old Charter and selected a Jubilee proclamation for the Assembly's Bell — with the exact anniversary motive still awaiting its strongest receipt.

Abolitionists received the Bell and judged the nation by words already on it.

Later movements received the renamed Bell again.

And now a quarter arrives.

The important inheritance may not be a finished answer.

It may be a **recoverable right to reopen the question**.

> **A PRIVILEGE THAT CANNOT SURVIVE A NEW RECEIVER IS ONLY A PERMISSION FROM THE PAST.**
>
> **A LIBERTY BECOMES DURABLE WHEN THE RECEIVER CAN STILL USE IT TO QUESTION POWER.**

That is the response.

Not "the founders solved liberty."

Almost the opposite.

The Charter under the Bell is a record of people discovering that if a liberty matters, it has to survive the departure of the person currently holding the keys.

## Epistemic split

**Documented fact:** The Charter strengthened the elected Assembly; protected a bounded form of liberty of conscience; contained a high amendment threshold and a special inviolability declaration for Article I; allowed Pennsylvania and the Lower Counties to separate legislatively while retaining other liberties; protected estates from forfeiture in specified suicide/accident cases; remained in force until the Revolution. Penn enslaved people. The State House Bell carries Leviticus 25:10 and was ordered in 1751. Abolitionists later generated the "Liberty Bell" identity from that inscription.

**Inference:** The Charter is usefully read as a continuity-under-departure instrument: a proprietor expecting absence and possible regime change yields enough local capacity that the political body can continue without his constant presence. The separation proviso is a materially independent historical specimen for continuity-through-fork rather than forced synchronization.

**Speculation / held:** Isaac Norris intentionally chose Leviticus 25:10 specifically because 1751 was the Charter's fiftieth anniversary. Institutional histories support this at differing confidence levels, but this slice has not yet recovered the direct 1751 witness needed to close the question. No claim is made that the U.S. Mint intended a five-Jubilee reading in 2026.
